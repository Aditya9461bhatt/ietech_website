Walk into most manufacturing companies and you'll find the same quiet standoff: the design office lives in CAD, the shop floor lives in job cards and WhatsApp photos, and the two meet somewhere in a folder called `FINAL_v7_USE_THIS_ONE`.

Every hour between "the drawing changed" and "the machine knows about it" is an hour of risk. Wrong revisions get machined. Tooling gets ordered against stale geometry. And nobody notices until the part is on the inspection table.

## What CAD integration actually means

It is not "everyone gets a CAD licence." Integration means your design data flows into the systems that plan and run production without a human re-typing it:

- **Bill of materials, straight from the model.** When the assembly changes, the BOM in your ERP changes with it — quantities, materials, bought-out parts.
- **Revisions with teeth.** A drawing revision blocks old work orders instead of politely coexisting with them.
- **Machine-readable handoff.** Geometry, tolerances, and operations reach CAM and the machines in a form they can use, not a printout.

## Where to start (without boiling the ocean)

We advise clients to sequence it in three steps:

1. **Single source of truth first.** Before any clever automation, decide where a drawing officially lives and make every other copy a reference to it. This costs almost nothing and kills the `FINAL_v7` problem on its own.
2. **Connect the BOM.** Wire your CAD structure into the ERP item master. This is where the compounding savings live — purchasing, costing, and planning all get more accurate at once.
3. **Then close the loop to the floor.** Work orders that reference live revisions, operators who see the current drawing on a screen next to the machine, and inspection results that flow back against the model.

## What it's worth

The teams we work with stop measuring "time spent looking for the right file" because it rounds to zero. Fewer wrong-revision parts, faster quoting (because the BOM and routing come from the model, not memory), and an engineering team that spends its week engineering.

If your drawings and your production system still communicate through printouts, that's the gap. It's very fixable — and it's usually the highest-return project on a manufacturer's digital list.
