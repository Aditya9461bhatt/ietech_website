"ERP" might be the worst-named product category in software. Enterprise Resource Planning sounds like something a multinational buys by the kilo. In reality, a manufacturing ERP answers three questions every factory owner asks daily:

- **Where is my material?**
- **Where are my orders?**
- **Where is my money?**

Everything else is detail. Here's the plain-language version of what sits under those questions.

## The core loop

A manufacturing ERP connects one chain of events that today probably lives in five places:

**Enquiry → quote → order → plan → purchase → produce → dispatch → invoice → payment.**

When that chain lives in one system, each step feeds the next automatically. The order creates the production plan. The plan checks stock and raises purchase requirements. Production consumes material and books machine time. Dispatch creates the invoice. The invoice chases its own payment. No re-typing, no reconciliation meetings.

## What changes in practice

- **Planning stops being archaeology.** You see load per machine and per week, so promising a delivery date becomes arithmetic instead of optimism.
- **Costing becomes real.** Material, labour, and overhead land on actual jobs — so you learn which products and customers genuinely make money.
- **The business survives absences.** Knowledge lives in the system, not in one indispensable person's workbook.
- **Audits and compliance get boring** — in the good way. GST, e-invoicing, and stock registers fall out of normal daily use.

## When you actually need one

Honest answer: later than software vendors say, earlier than most owners act. The reliable trigger points we see:

1. You're **quoting slower than competitors** because costing takes days.
2. **Stock inaccuracy** is causing either idle machines or bloated inventory.
3. **Month-end reconciliation** takes your accountant a week.
4. You're **adding a second unit, shift, or product line** and coordination is breaking.

If none of those are true yet, keep your spreadsheets and invest in process discipline — we say this to prospects regularly.

## Why "configured" beats "customised" (and both beat "template")

A template ERP forces your plant to work the way the software imagines. Heavy customisation builds you a one-off nobody can maintain. The middle path — a strong core *configured* around your routings, approval chains, and documents — is where deployments succeed.
